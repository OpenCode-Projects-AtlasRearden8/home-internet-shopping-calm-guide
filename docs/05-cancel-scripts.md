# 05 · Cancel Scripts: Disagreeing Politely (and Winning)

*The retention agent is a person doing a job, not a villain. Your script
works because it's calm, not because it's sneaky.*

Four situations, four scripts. Written to be read out loud, word for word —
that's the whole magic. Reading a script prevents the tone drift that turns
phone calls into spirals.

## Before any call (2 minutes)

Have in front of you:
- Account number, last payment amount/date
- The date you want service on the *new* plan (if switching)
- Your "walk-away number": the monthly total under which you'd happily stay
- Something to write on: names, dates, amounts

Good-to-know facts (varies; confirm locally): some states allow **cancellation
rights windows** after a price change; **military deployment/hardship** rules
can waive ETFs; providers must typically honor **confirmed confirmation
numbers** as records. Ask for the confirmation number *every time*, and for
email confirmation in writing.

## Script 1 — Cancel cleanly (no negotiation wanted)

> "Hi, calling to cancel my internet service, effective [date].
> I'm not looking to discuss offers — I've settled on a new provider.
> Can you please process the cancellation, confirm no device-fee charges
> after return, and give me a confirmation number and the address for
> returning the equipment?"

**If they switch you to retention:** that's their job, not a trap.

> "I appreciate you checking for offers — genuinely. My decision's made.
> Could you cancel exactly as I described? Take your time."

Politeness loops, unchanged words, a good 2–3 times ends most calls. If
fired up again, add variety:

> "You've been helpful. Please note on my account that I've requested
> cancellation twice today, effective [date], and read me the
> confirmation number."

Then the confirmation number — **always** get it before hanging up.

## Script 2 — Cancel *or* get a real discount (the two-way door)

Use when you'd stay for the right price. The magic of this script is that
it's honest about the either-or:

> "Hi, I have my bill here. After [price X] this month it jumps to
> [price Y]. My plan is to switch to [competitor/new provider] at
> [their number]. Before I do that, I wanted to give you the chance to
> keep me: if you can get my monthly total — equipment included — down
> to around [$ walk-away number], I'll happily stay. Otherwise I'd like
> to cancel at the end of this billing cycle."

Behavior notes:
- **Silence is a tool**: ask and then *wait*. They go look at things. Wait
  longer than feels comfortable (count to 30 slowly).
- **They'll offer a tier you don't want.** "That's a slower plan than I'll
  take, but thank you — is there a price for the tier I have now?"
- **They'll offer a 6-month promo.** Answer with arithmetic:
  "I'm going by the 24-month average — what can you do that includes
  month 25?"
- Accept, decline, or cancel in whichever order the conversation lands.
  All three endings are wins; the sheet told you your number.

## Script 3 — Cancelling because service was bad (documented)

Do *not* open with anger, do open with the log:

> "Starting to cancellation today because of [n/the] repeat evening
> slowdowns. Just so it's in the notes: on [date1] I ran speed tests at
> [X] Mbps on a plan advertised at [Y] Mbps, and had a ticket #00 set on
> [date2], plus [date3]. I gave it [months] to improve — it didn't.
> I'd like to cancel without an early-termination fee, as [advertised
> speed has not been delivered / service was unavailable some
> evenings]. If policy requires supervisor approval for the fee
> waiver, please do that — I'm happy to wait."

That's not a threat; it's a receipt-shaped request. Fee-waiver approvals
often work exactly this way: calm notes + ticket numbers + patience while
they escalate.

## Script 4 — Cancelling a contract early (without panic)

> "I'm on a term agreement that ends [date]. I need to cancel as of
> [date] because [reason: move / military orders per my eligibility /
> unacceptable price jump]. Could you tell me (1) the exact early
> termination fee for [date] per my agreement, (2) every exception my
> situation might fall under, and (3) the prorating options?"

Ask the three, keep the answers, take the cheapest path. If the move is
within the provider's footprint, ask whether a "**transfer of service**"
avoids the fee — many pay for nothing at that point. This one has saved people
real amounts.

## The equipment-return epilogue (where money hides)

Unreturned equipment fees are silent, slow, and real. Calm checklist:

- Return within the stated window (commonly 30 days) — *and get a receipt
  or tracking number* if you mail it.
- Photograph the equipment and the store drop-off receipt; two photos per
  box, saved to your phone's "Bills" album or your paperwork folder.
- Calendar "check final bill landed correctly" ~40 days after cancellation.
  If an equipment fee appears despite your receipt — the next sentence is
  the script:

> "I have a return receipt from [date] — can I email it to you and have
> the unreturned-equipment fee removed? Confirmation number, please."

## Script 5 — The polite handshake when *ordering* the new service

The mirror script. (Prevention beats dispute.)

> "Confirming order [#.]: plan [name], month 1–24 prices [$X then $Y],
> equipment fee [$], installation type [self-install / professional],
> contract [none / terms], and total one-time charges today. Can I get
> this summary in an email? Perfect, thank you."

That email, saved, converts "future disputes" into "past drafts." The
theme of this whole guide: **every confirmation number is a small lock,
and locks help you be calm.**

## When the call just goes badly

Some calls misfire: dropped, transferred endlessly, promised thing A,
delivered thing B. The restorative move is:
1. Hang up kindly.
2. Try again later (a different agent is a fresh roll).
3. Use the provider's online chat with the *same* script — chat gives you
   a written transcript for free.
4. Escalate in writing (public postmortems, regulators) *only after* two
   in-account attempts. Usually not needed.

## Where to stop for today

If you have one call to make, do it with one script. If procrastination is
louder than duty today (it happens — we designed this guide around that
tendency), the compromise task is: fill in the "walk-away number" line on
your [comparison sheet](06-printable-sheets.md) and schedule the call for a
named time tomorrow. That's real progress; no shame allowed.
