# 04 · Lower-Cost Plans: Pay Less Without Losing Sanity

*The best available price is often not the one on the homepage. Here's the
quiet list, in the order sanity would check it.*

Educational guide: programs, names, and eligibility rules change — always
confirm current details on the provider's or program's own site before
deciding. We keep company names out of the fine print and verbs in.

## The order to check things (top is usually the biggest win)

### 1. Ask for "the current customer offers" at your *existing* provider
Retention and loyalty teams frequently hold price forgiveness out of habit —
only you have to interrupt them to get it. The script for this lives in
[Guide 05](05-cancel-scripts.md) (the "cancel-then-win" maneuver works
surprisingly often *without* ever finishing the cancel sentence).

Expected outcomes, honestly ranged:
- several dollars off the equipment fee
- a promo-rate re-up for 12 months
- a mid-tier speed bump for roughly the price you already pay
- "no, sorry" — which is fine, that's the queue for step 2

### 2. Lower the speed tier on purpose
Guide 01's table says most 1–2 person households are fine at 100–300 Mbps.
If you're paying gigabit rates and living a 300 Mbps life, dropping a tier
often saves real money with zero felt difference. Do the three speed tests
first so you're downgrading from knowledge, not guesswork.

### 3. Bundle nothing (check standalone pricing)
Streaming-era math favors standalone internet + streaming-only services you
actually open. Run "single service total24" vs "bundle total24." Bundles win
occasionally; both should be on the sheet.

### 4. Buy your equipment
Guide 03's table: rental ≈ $60–$180 per year. A good own-router pays back in
1–2 years. If your provider publishes a compatibility list (most do), pick a
$50–$80 modem from it and keep the rental fee's cousin, the equipment fee,
off your bill forever.

### 5. Low-income & qualifying programs (the unsung section)
Many providers run their own low-income tier programs; requirements commonly
include participation in assistance programs (SNAP/SSI/housing aid and
similar) or education benefits. Additionally, there are national/lifeline-
style programs that reduce phone *or* home internet bills for qualifying
households; utilities' community outreach programs sometimes have their own
internet subsidy one-pagers. Search phrases that work well:

- "[provider name] low income internet program"
- "[your state] lifeline internet program"
- "[your county] library community internet assistance"

**No shame in these. They're building on purpose.** Eligibility renews
annually; calendar it.

### 6. Neighborhood co-bargaining (soft slow-burn option)
Bulk discounts for multi-tenant buildings are a real thing; property managers
sometimes just need someone to ask. If you rent and you're friendly, one
polite note to your landlord ("any group rate for internet?") occasionally
moves a building. Outcomes vary; cost of asking is one email.

### 7. The switch-and-return cycle (play it ethically)
Many providers run promo pricing for *new* customers only; moving out and
name-switching legitimately creates "new customer" status. If a household
member (spouse/partner/roommate) opens the account in their own name, you're
frequently eligible for new-customer promos. This is fully above-board; ask
the rep "what's the current promo for new accounts?" and read the fine print
on the promo terms.

### 8. Prepaid plans (the sleep-easy special)
Some providers offer month-to-month prepaid internet: pay-as-you-go, no
credit check, no autopay hostage, no contract. Prices sometimes *look*
higher-but-honest because there's no intro teaser and no surprise fees.
Great if your income is irregular or you want the *total* price visible in
one number. On the comparison sheet, prepaid's "Month 13–24 price" equals
month 1 — a rare comfort.

## The classic missteps (all forgivable, then avoidable)

1. **Chasing the cheapest advertised tier without checking upload.** Cheap
   tiers sometimes have 5–10 Mbps upload: perfectly fine for one working
   adult, punished for two simultaneous video calls.
2. **Skipping autopay because of fear and losing the autopay discount.**
   Compromise: dedicated debit card with a low balance.
3. **Forgetting the equipment fee** in mental math. $10 × 24 = $240, which
   funds a nice router *and* a pizza.
4. **Self-installing when you needed a technician** (old wall jacks die). If
   self-install doesn't work within an hour of honest trying, book a legit
   tech; fees often refundable on request when the self-install path was
   misrepresented as compatible. Politely ask.
5. **Annual-spend blindness.** Run the Annual math on the sheet: monthly
   × 12. A $60/mo plan is $720/year, which *is* the price of nice plane
   tickets. You're allowed to care.

## The comparison-in-one-breath

When you're down to two offers and tired, ask these 4 questions:

1. Which one's **Avg24** is lower? (Guide 02)
2. Which has more **upload**? (Guide 01)
3. Which has fewer **fees**? (Guide 02)
4. Which am I less afraid of **calling** if something breaks? (Guide 05)

Then choose that one and close 37 tabs. There is no perfect plan, only one
you understood fully at purchase.

## Where to stop for today

Run steps 1–2 (the two fastest wins). If you have energy for one more, do
the eligibility search in step 5. Write results on the sheet; tomorrow is a
fine day for the rest.
